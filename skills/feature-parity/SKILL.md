# Feature Parity

Use this skill when the user wants to learn from leading peers, benchmark a
business or product, or reach feature parity with a proven competitor.

Feature parity means matching the useful customer capability or operating
outcome. It does not mean copying source code, proprietary data, trademarks,
brand identity, protected assets, or distinctive wording.

## Stage 1: Find the right parity target

Inspect the project's current product, customer, goals, evidence, and constraints
before researching peers. Use current primary sources and dated third-party
evidence where it materially improves confidence.

Research a small credible set of direct peers and useful adjacent leaders. For
each candidate, distinguish:

- evidence of success, such as adoption, revenue, verified outcomes, customer
  reviews, durable usage, or scale;
- fit with this project's target customer, business model, and resources;
- the customer-visible capabilities or operating patterns worth transferring;
- claims that are self-published, ambiguous, stale, or unsupported;
- the cost and risk of reaching parity.

Do not call a peer the most successful without comparable evidence. If public
evidence cannot support a clear ranking, label the ranking uncertain and explain
which definition of success would change it.

Present a compact comparison, recommend one primary parity target and at most one
secondary reference, then ask the user to confirm the target. Stop before
creating a parity plan, editing the project, purchasing anything, or changing an
external system.

## Stage 2: Build the parity map

Resume only after the user selects or confirms the target. Verify the target is
still current, then compare the project with it across the dimensions that matter
for the specific business. Typical dimensions include:

- core customer journey and activation;
- essential product or service capabilities;
- trust, proof, safety, and human controls;
- offer structure, pricing presentation, and ownership terms;
- delivery operations, integrations, monitoring, and support;
- retention, renewal, and expansion loops.

Classify each target capability as `match`, `partial`, `missing`, `not relevant`,
or `blocked by evidence`. Separate features directly observed from inferences.
Identify the peer's core value loop and the smallest set of gaps that would make
the project credibly competitive for its chosen customer.

Do not treat parity as a reason to import every feature. Exclude capabilities
that depend on a different customer, scale, regulation, business model, or
unverified claim.

## Stage 3: Close gaps in value order

Recommend the parity work in this order:

1. capabilities required to complete the customer's core outcome;
2. trust, safety, and evidence needed to make the outcome credible;
3. activation and usability gaps that block real adoption;
4. retention and operating capabilities;
5. optional breadth.

For each item, state the customer outcome, parity evidence, acceptance criteria,
dependencies, and what cannot be solved by implementation alone. Preserve
existing work and reuse completed capabilities.

When the user asks to implement, complete the highest-value safe slice, verify
observable behavior, and update the parity map. Do not fabricate outcomes or
represent local work as live. Do not purchase services, deploy, publish claims,
contact peers or customers, or make other external mutations without the
authority required for that action.

## Durable artifact

After the user confirms a target, maintain `FEATURE-PARITY.md` in the project if
the comparison will span more than one session. Record the selected peer, dated
sources, success definition, capability matrix, excluded features, current
priority, evidence gaps, and last verified status. Keep temporary research notes
out of the durable artifact.
