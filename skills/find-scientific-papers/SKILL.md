# Find Scientific Papers

Turn the current situation into answerable research questions, find verified papers, and explain what the evidence changes about the decision. Work across topics and chats without depending on a particular repository, personal vault, or remembered biography.

## Frame the question

Read the current conversation first. Infer the practical question and desired outcome; state that interpretation briefly. If several concerns are present, prioritize the active decision and separate distinct questions. Ask a concise clarification only when the missing detail materially changes the search; otherwise proceed with stated assumptions.

Translate everyday language into searchable constructs. For example, finding friends in a new city may involve repeated interaction, belonging, social networks and shared activities. Separate empirical questions from preferences or values: papers can inform tradeoffs but cannot determine someone's ideal life. Do not assume a diagnosis or psychological cause from a casual description.

## Find and verify evidence

Use available web search or scholarly tools for a fresh search. Choose sources appropriate to the field: PubMed, Europe PMC, Cochrane, scholarly indexes, journal or publisher sites, conference proceedings, institutional repositories, and preprint archives. These are options, not required dependencies. Keep identifying and sensitive personal details out of public search queries; search general research concepts.

Start with relevant systematic reviews or meta-analyses when available, then inspect important primary studies, recent updates, replications, and contradictory or null results. Match study design to the question: randomized interventions for causal effects when feasible, longitudinal studies for change over time, qualitative studies for lived experience, and appropriate technical benchmarks for engineering questions. Do not force a medical evidence hierarchy onto every field. Prefer relevance and methodological quality over novelty, citation counts or famous authors.

Verify each included paper's title, authors, year, publication venue and DOI or stable source URL. Open its authoritative record and read the full text when available. Label abstract-only or secondary-only access and limit claims accordingly. Never invent citations, sample sizes, effect estimates or conclusions. Search snippets are discovery aids, not sufficient evidence for detailed claims. Distinguish peer-reviewed papers, preprints, protocols and commentary. Check visible corrections, retractions and newer contradictory evidence for central papers. If tools or full text are unavailable, say what could not be verified.

## Judge what the research actually supports

For each important finding, consider:

- Study design, population, setting, sample size and follow-up period.
- Effect size and uncertainty when reported and interpretable, not just statistical significance. Separate absolute from relative effects.
- Confounding, selection bias, attrition, measurement quality, publication bias, funding or conflicts where relevant.
- Whether the measured outcome matches the user's goal; short-term proxies may not predict lasting results.
- Whether the population and setting plausibly transfer to this situation. Correlation, animal findings and laboratory results do not automatically establish real-world human causation.
- Whether apparently independent reviews reuse the same studies. More citations do not necessarily mean more independent evidence.

Distinguish established findings, tentative explanations and your own inference. Give a plain-language confidence judgment with reasons, not an invented formal evidence grade. Report mixed or absent evidence honestly; absence of evidence is not proof of no effect. Do not promise certainty, diagnose the user, or turn general studies into personalized treatment instructions.

## Return a useful answer

Lead with the practical takeaway and its confidence. Usually select three to six strong, directly relevant papers rather than an exhaustive bibliography; adapt to the request and evidence. For each, provide a linked title, author/year, study type, main finding and the key limitation or relevance to this situation. Put links beside supported claims and prefer a freely accessible full-text version when available.

Then explain:

- What the evidence supports doing or considering.
- What remains unknown, contested or dependent on the user's preferences.
- One small, proportionate next step or low-risk experiment when useful, with an observable outcome. Label it as an application of the research, not a proven personal prescription.

Briefly state search date, sources or search terms, and scope limits so the search can be continued. Do not claim a systematic or exhaustive review unless that method was actually performed. Scientific rigor should clarify the decision, not become another obstacle to acting.

Finding papers does not authorize changing calendars, contacting people, publishing personal context, or rewriting memory. Save a research note only when requested or already authorized, using the appropriate private location. Do not create files merely because the skill is invoked.
